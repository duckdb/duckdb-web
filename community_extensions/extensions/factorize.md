    -- counts from it, so a result too large to materialise is still countable.
    -- With factorize_mode='auto' an ordinary count(*) is taken over only where

      FROM edges a, edges b, edges c, edges d
     WHERE a.src = b.src AND b.src = c.src AND c.src = d.src;

  extended_description: |
    A clean-room implementation of Lehner & Neumann, *The Data World Is Not
    Flat: Efficient Factorized Execution for Relational Systems* (PVLDB
    19(11):3006-3019, 2026), derived from the paper only.

    A join's result can be exponentially larger than the data it comes from. A
    factorised representation keeps it as a product of independent parts rather
    than writing every combination out: records grow by sum where tuples grow
    by product. For an aggregate that only needs the count, the count can be
    read from that representation without ever materialising it.

    **What it is for.** Queries whose join result is too large to produce. On
    the 442 queries of the cardinality-estimation benchmark whose results exceed
    1e9 tuples, this answers 244 `count(\*)` queries that stock DuckDB does not
    finish in sixty seconds, in a median of 0.09 s. One of them counts 10.8
    trillion results in 24 seconds where the stock plan produces nothing in 180.

    That 244 is specific to `count(\*)`, and deliberately says so: which queries
    stock DuckDB cannot finish depends on the aggregate, not only on the join.
    Over a random 120-query sample of the same corpus, with both plans timed
    and a sixty-second cap, stock finishes 38 to 41 of them once the fold is
    not `count(\*)` -- about a third of the corpus stops being out of reach.

    The rescues survive the change of aggregate, which had been assumed rather
    than measured until it was:

        fold          rescued   faster   slower   neither   lost
        min                45       34        7        34      0
        max                47       33        7        33      0
        avg                45       31       10        34      0
        sum                46       31        9        33      1
        count(c)           59       29       13        18      1
        sum(x * y)         46       30       10        34      0

    "Neither" is the floor neither plan clears inside the cap. count(c) reaches
    furthest because the matcher only accepts it where the column cannot be
    NULL, which makes the fold a counter again.

    The slower column is mostly not this engine. Splitting the same runs by
    whether the gate fired, every one of those slowdowns but two sits among
    the queries it *declined* -- where `auto` runs the stock plan and the
    difference is two timings of the same work, median ratio 0.98 over 151
    pairs. Across 458 fired queries there are two bad outcomes, both under
    count(c): one is the 60s cap falling just past a 58.8s stock plan, and the
    other is watdiv_acyclic_217_10, which this project has recorded the cost
    model failing on since well before these measurements.

    Where it does regress is the corpus it is not for. On the queries DuckDB
    can already materialise, about a third of the queries the gate fires on
    come out slower -- 13 of the 37 it fires on for count(\*). And `auto` costs about 2 to 4 milliseconds to decline -- the
    statistics sampling behind the decision -- which is invisible past 100ms
    and around 20% on a 10ms query.

    **What it is not for.** Queries DuckDB already handles. For a plain
    count(\*) DuckDB carries no payload columns through a join and counts empty
    tuples faster than this engine can build a representation: firing on every
    eligible query is about 11x *slower* on the benchmark's live corpus. The
    cost model exists to tell those regimes apart, and `auto` declines far more
    often than it fires.

    **Scope.** count(\*), count(c), sum, avg, min, max, bool_and and bool_or
    over acyclic equi-join
    graphs of many-to-many joins on integer and DECIMAL columns, with GROUP BY,
    EXISTS and LIMIT. Also sum(x * y) for two columns, ungrouped, which is the
    case factorisation is for: over independent branches it decomposes into a
    product of one-column sums, so the pairings of x and y are summed without
    being enumerated even though they are the join result itself.
    Anything else is declined and the stock plan runs unchanged. `auto` is
    bit-exact against `off`; if the factorised path raises, the replaced plan
    answers instead.

    The aggregates beyond count(\*) are timed as well as verified (D81). The
    gate prices min and max separately from the rest, because they are not
    folded at all: an ungrouped min or max over an acyclic join is answered by
    a semijoin reducer, which reads the inputs and never builds the join. So
    the folds no longer fire on the same queries as each other, which they did
    until the reducer got a cost model of its own.

    On the benchmark's live corpus, over the same 119 join graphs, medians of
    three runs each:

        fold          fires   ratio
        min              65    3.9x
        max              65    3.7x
        count(\*)        37    1.7x
        count(c)         36    1.4x
        avg              29    1.5x
        sum(x * y)       29    1.5x
        sum              29    1.5x

    Single runs of this measurement vary by about 10%, so these are medians
    and not precise quantities. bool_and and bool_or are not listed separately:
    the matcher compiles them to the min and max folds with the same binding,
    so they are those rows with a BOOLEAN result type. An earlier reading of 0.93x for
    sum(x * y) -- "slower than not firing" -- was an artifact: 65 of the 119
    queries in that corpus multiplied two INTEGER join keys whose product
    overflows INT32, so they raised instead of answering, and the gate fired on
    30 of those against 3 of the rest. It measured how fast each plan reached
    the same error (D85, D86). In the regime these exist for: on 50 of the
    excluded queries sum(x * y) is 7 faster against 1 slower with no losses,
    221s of stock against 92s.

    **Known limitations**, measured rather than assumed, with the numbers in
    DECISIONS.md: the cost model's coefficients are fitted on one machine and do
    not transfer between machines or datasets; its estimates are wrong where it
    is most confident; and on the benchmark corpus a handful of queries run
    slower than the plan they replace, one of them past a sixty-second cap.
    Scored against a per-query oracle with both plans timed, the gate lands
    within about 1.1x to 1.4x of perfect depending on the aggregate, and most
    of what is left is one query.

    The counts of rescued queries above were measured before the semijoin
    reducer and before the cost model was refitted for it, both of which only
    widened what gets answered, so they understate the current state rather
    than overstating it. DECISIONS.md carries each measurement with the date
    and method it was taken by.
